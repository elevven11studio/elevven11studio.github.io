How to Separate Business and Personal Money

23 Nov 2026 · 4 min read

"The business is doing well, but I don't know where the money goes." If that sounds familiar, the problem is often not sales. It is that the business money and your own money live in the same account, the same purse and the same phone.

When they are mixed, you cannot tell how much the business really makes. You also cannot tell how much you are taking out of it.

Why it matters

Keeping them apart helps in ways you feel quickly:

  • You see the real profit. When personal spending is mixed in, a good business can look like a struggling one, or the other way round.
  • You stop draining the business by accident. Small withdrawals for food, data and transport add up, and suddenly there is no money for stock.
  • Your records make sense. If you ever need a loan, a partner or an accountant, they will ask for clean business records.
  • Arguments end. In a family business, separate money means fewer disagreements about who spent what.

Step 1: Open a separate account

Use one bank account for the business only. All business income goes in and all business expenses go out of it. Nothing else.

If you can, open the account in your business name. Ask your bank what documents they need. If that is not possible yet, a second personal account used only for the business is still far better than one shared account.

Do the same for your phone wallets and POS terminal. Customer payments should land in the business account, not your personal one.

Step 2: Keep the cash apart too

Many small businesses take a lot of cash. Give it the same treatment:

  • Keep a separate cash box or drawer for the business.
  • Do not pay personal bills from it.
  • Bank the takings regularly, so the business account shows what you actually sold.

If you take cash for yourself, write it down every time. "Took NGN 5,000 for transport" in the notebook is better than no record at all.

Step 3: Pay yourself a fixed amount

This is the step most owners skip, and the one that makes the biggest difference. Instead of taking money whenever you need it, pay yourself a set amount on a set day, like a salary.

How to choose the amount:

  1. Look at your profit over the last three to six months.
  2. Take the average.
  3. Pay yourself less than that average, so the business keeps some for stock, repairs and slow months.

For example, if your business made an average of NGN 200,000 profit a month, you might pay yourself NGN 120,000 and leave NGN 80,000 in the business. That is 60% to you and 40% kept back.

Pay it on the same date every month, by transfer from the business account to your personal one. If the business has a great month, you can pay yourself a bonus later. If it has a bad month, you will be glad money was left behind.

Step 4: Stop paying personal costs from the business

Once the salary is in place, personal costs come out of your personal account. School fees, family support, groceries and outings are paid from your salary, not the business.

If you must use business money for something personal, treat it as part of your pay for that month and record it. Do not let it become a habit.

Step 5: Record every business transaction

Separate accounts help, but you still need records. For each sale and each expense, note the date, amount and what it was for. Keep receipts and invoices for business purchases.

A simple monthly check:

QuestionWhere to look
How much came in?Business account plus cash takings
How much went out on the business?Business account and receipts
How much did I pay myself?Transfers to your personal account
How much is left?Business account balance and cash box

If you are not sure what to record, read how to keep simple business records.

Make the switch without stress

You do not need to sort out years of history first. Pick a start date, such as the first day of next month, and separate from that day forward.

On that date:

  • Count the business cash and move it to the business account or cash box.
  • Tell regular customers and suppliers which account to use.
  • Set up your monthly salary transfer.

The first month feels strict. By the third, you will know exactly what the business makes and what you take, which is the whole point.

Get advice for the bigger questions

Questions about registering a business, tax and what records the law expects depend on your situation. Speak to an accountant for those. Clean, separate records will make that conversation quicker and cheaper.

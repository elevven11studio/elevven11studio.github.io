How to Track Letters In and Out of Your Office

23 Nov 2026 · 4 min read

A letter from a client arrives. Someone signs for it, puts it on a desk, and two weeks later the client calls asking why nobody replied. Nobody can find the letter. Nobody is sure who was supposed to handle it.

Offices that deal with clients, regulators, landlords or government agencies live on letters. A simple correspondence register stops them getting lost and makes sure each one gets answered.

What a correspondence register is

It is a list of every letter that comes into or goes out of the office, in order, with enough detail to find the letter and know what happened to it. Many offices keep two: an incoming register and an outgoing register. It can be a hardbound book, a spreadsheet or software.

What matters is the rule: no letter enters or leaves without being registered.

What to record for each letter

Incoming letters

Our reference numberA unique number you can quote and file by
Date receivedShows how long it has been waiting
SenderWho it is from
Sender's referenceThe number they will quote when they follow up
SubjectA few words, so it can be searched
Who acts on itOne named person is responsible
Reply due byThe date a response should go out
Where it is filedThe file or folder it ends up in

Outgoing letters

Record your reference, the date sent, the recipient, the subject, who signed it and, if it is a reply, which incoming letter it answers.

Number every letter

Give incoming and outgoing letters separate running numbers with a short prefix, for example IN-0001 and OUT-0001. Put the number on the letter itself, top right, as soon as it is registered.

Numbers never get reused, even for letters that turn out to be unimportant. A gap in the sequence should always have an explanation.

Link replies to what they answer

The most useful habit is writing down which letter a reply answers. When your outgoing letter OUT-0047 replies to IN-0112, note it on both. Later, anyone can follow the whole conversation in order without digging through a cabinet.

Set a reply date and check it

Every incoming letter that needs an answer should get a reply-by date when it is registered. Then, once a week, someone goes through the register and asks one question: which letters are past their reply date with no reply recorded?

That short weekly check is what turns a register from a list into a control.

Scan and file the original

Scan each letter when it arrives and keep the paper in the right file. The scan lets people read it without moving the original. The paper file stays where your archive says it is. If you need a system for the paper side, read how to organize paper files in your office.

Common mistakes

  • Letters handed over at reception and never registered. Make reception the single entry point.
  • "Everyone" is responsible. Name one person for each letter.
  • Replies sent without a reference. The other side cannot match your answer to their letter.
  • No weekly check. A register nobody reviews will not catch late replies.

Start this week

  1. Pick one entry point for all incoming letters.
  2. Start a numbered register today. Do not try to back-fill years of old letters first.
  3. Give every letter that needs an answer a named person and a reply date.
  4. Check for late replies every Monday.

Within a month, "we never got your letter" stops being something your office says.

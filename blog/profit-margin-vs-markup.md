# Profit Margin vs Markup: What Is the Difference?

Margin and markup use the same profit but measure it against different numbers. Worked examples in Naira and how to price for the margin you want.

Two traders buy the same bag of rice for NGN 40,000 and sell it for NGN 50,000. One says she makes 25% on it. The other says he makes 20%. They are both right. One is talking about markup and the other about margin.

Mixing the two up is one of the most common pricing mistakes in small businesses, and it usually means earning less than you planned.

## Same profit, different base

Both numbers start with the same profit:

- **Profit** = selling price minus cost

The difference is what you divide that profit by:

- **Markup** = profit divided by **cost**, times 100
- **Margin** = profit divided by **selling price**, times 100

Markup tells you how much you added on top of what you paid. Margin tells you how much of each sale you keep. Because the selling price is always bigger than the cost when you make a profit, the margin is always the smaller of the two.

## Worked example: the bag of rice

Cost NGN 40,000, selling price NGN 50,000.

1. Profit is 50,000 minus 40,000, which is **NGN 10,000**.
2. Markup is 10,000 divided by 40,000, which is 0.25, so **25%**.
3. Margin is 10,000 divided by 50,000, which is 0.20, so **20%**.

## Worked example: phone cases

You buy phone cases at NGN 2,000 each and sell them at NGN 3,000. You sell 10 in a week.

1. Revenue is 3,000 times 10, which is NGN 30,000.
2. Total cost is 2,000 times 10, which is NGN 20,000.
3. Profit is NGN 10,000.
4. Markup is 10,000 divided by 20,000, so **50%**.
5. Margin is 10,000 divided by 30,000, so **33.3%**.

A 50% markup sounds healthy, but only a third of the money coming in is profit. The rest pays back what the cases cost you.

## How markup and margin line up

| Markup | Margin |
|---|---|
| 25% | 20% |
| 50% | 33.3% |
| 100% | 50% |
| 200% | 66.7% |

To convert between them, use the percentages as decimals:

- Margin = markup divided by (1 + markup). For a 50% markup: 0.5 divided by 1.5 is 0.333, so 33.3%.
- Markup = margin divided by (1 minus margin). For a 20% margin: 0.2 divided by 0.8 is 0.25, so 25%.

Notice that margin can never reach 100%, but markup can go as high as you like. Selling at three times the cost is a 200% markup.

## The mistake that costs money

Say you know you need a 30% margin to cover rent, staff and transport. An item costs you NGN 10,000, so you add 30% and sell it at NGN 13,000.

Your profit is NGN 3,000. Divide that by the selling price of 13,000 and your margin is **23.1%**, not 30%. You priced with markup when your target was a margin.

To hit a 30% margin, divide the cost by (1 minus the margin):

- 10,000 divided by 0.70 is NGN 14,285.71. Round it to NGN 14,290.

Check it: profit is 14,290 minus 10,000, which is 4,290. And 4,290 divided by 14,290 is 30.0%. That is the price you needed.

## Which one should you use?

Use **markup** when you set prices from cost. It is quick: take what you paid and add a percentage.

Use **margin** when you plan the business. Your expenses and profit targets come out of sales, so margin tells you whether your prices actually cover them. Lenders and accountants usually talk in margins too.

The safest habit is to look at both. Set a price with markup, then check the margin it gives you.

## Include every cost

Both numbers are only as good as the cost you start with. If you pay for delivery, packaging, bank charges or a market levy on each item, add them to the cost before you calculate. Leaving them out makes every percentage look better than it is.

# Cash Flow Basics for Small Business Owners

Why a profitable business can still run out of cash, how to track money in and out each week, and how credit sales and reserves change the picture.

A business can make a profit and still be unable to pay rent. It sounds wrong, but it happens all the time. The reason is the difference between profit and cash.

Profit is what you earned on paper. Cash is what is actually in the drawer and the bank today. You pay suppliers, staff and landlords with cash, not profit.

## Cash vs profit: a worked example

Here is a month at a small provisions shop.

- Sales: **NGN 500,000**. Of that, NGN 150,000 was sold on credit to regular customers who have not paid yet.
- The goods sold cost the shop NGN 350,000.
- Rent and other expenses: NGN 50,000.

On paper, profit is 500,000 minus 350,000 minus 50,000, which is **NGN 100,000**. A good month.

Now the cash. Only NGN 350,000 actually came in, because NGN 150,000 is still owed. During the month the owner paid NGN 350,000 to restock and NGN 50,000 for rent, so NGN 400,000 went out.

Cash in minus cash out is 350,000 minus 400,000. The shop is **NGN 50,000 down** in cash, in a month that made NGN 100,000 profit.

Nothing was stolen and nothing was priced wrong. The money is sitting in customers' pockets.

## What drains cash

The common causes are almost always the same:

- **Credit sales.** You hand over stock today and get paid later, sometimes much later.
- **Buying too much stock.** Goods on the shelf are cash you cannot spend.
- **Big payments that come at once.** Rent paid yearly, school fees, equipment, festive season stock.
- **Taking money out for personal use** without tracking it.
- **Slow-moving items** that tie up money for months.

## Track money in and out every week

You do not need accounting software to start. A notebook or a simple spreadsheet works. Once a week, write down:

| Item | Amount (NGN) |
|---|---|
| Cash at the start of the week | |
| Money in: cash and transfer sales | |
| Money in: debts customers paid | |
| Money out: stock | |
| Money out: rent, salaries, bills | |
| Money out: anything else | |
| Cash at the end of the week | |

The end figure should match what you actually have in the drawer and the bank. If it does not, something is missing from the record.

After a few weeks you will see patterns. Which weeks are slow, when big bills land, and how much cash you usually need to get through a month.

## Look ahead, not only back

Once you have a few weeks of records, plan the next month. List every payment you know is coming and when: rent, salaries, supplier invoices, loan repayments. Then estimate the money coming in.

If a week shows more going out than coming in, you see it early and can act. Collect debts sooner, delay a stock order, or move a payment date before it becomes a crisis.

## Control credit sales

Credit is the biggest cash trap for small shops. You do not have to stop it, but you do need limits and records. Set a maximum for each customer, agree on payment dates and follow up on time.

## Build a cash reserve

A reserve is money you set aside and do not touch for normal spending. It covers a slow month, a broken freezer or a supplier who suddenly wants cash upfront.

How to start:

1. Work out your fixed monthly costs: rent, salaries, power, data.
2. Set a target, such as one month of those costs, and later more.
3. Move a fixed amount into the reserve every week, even if it is small.
4. Keep it in a separate account so it is not mixed with daily takings.

If you dip into it, refill it before you build anything else.

## Know your break-even point

Your break-even point is the sales you need just to cover your costs. Knowing it tells you how much of each month is survival and how much is real profit. Read [how to find your break-even point](https://elevven11studio.github.io/blog/how-to-find-your-break-even-point/), or work it out with the free [break-even calculator](https://elevven11studio.github.io/tools/break-even-calculator/).

## Keep business and personal cash apart

It is hard to see your cash flow when the business money and your own money share one pocket. Separating them is one of the simplest fixes. See [how to separate business and personal money](https://elevven11studio.github.io/blog/separate-business-and-personal-money/).

For bigger decisions, such as taking a loan or planning tax, speak to an accountant. They can look at your actual numbers and advise you properly.

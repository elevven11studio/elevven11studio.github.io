# How to Spot a Fake Login Page for Your Bank or Email

The signs that a bank, email or payment login page is fake, why a password manager that will not fill is a clue, and what to do after a slip.

A fake login page has one job: to get you to type something that lets a stranger into your account. It might copy your bank, your work email, a payment service or a delivery company asking for a small fee. The design is usually perfect, because copying a page is easy.

What a fake page cannot do well is behave exactly like the real service. That is where you catch it.

## Your password manager is a quiet alarm

If you use a password manager, including the one built into Chrome, Android or iPhone, pay attention when it does **not** offer to fill your details. Password managers match saved logins to the exact website they were saved on. On a lookalike domain they see a different site and stay silent.

So if your bank login usually fills itself and today it does not, stop. Do not copy the password out of the manager and paste it in. Check the address first. There are innocent reasons, such as the bank moving its login to a new address, but a sudden change is worth a minute of checking.

This is one of the best reasons to use a password manager at all. It checks the address more carefully than a tired person does.

## Signs by type of page

### Bank login pages

- It asks for everything at once: username, password, PIN, card number and OTP on one screen. Real banks split these and never ask for your full PIN.
- It asks you to "verify" or "update" details through a link you were sent.
- After you log in, it says there was an error and asks you to enter your card details again.

### Email login pages

- You were sent a "shared document" or "voicemail" and must sign in to see it.
- The page already shows your email address and only asks for the password. Scammers fill this in to make it feel familiar.
- The page is a generic sign in box with a logo, sitting on an address that has nothing to do with your email provider.

### Payment and checkout pages

- A message about a parcel, a fine or a refund leads to a page asking for card details to pay a tiny fee.
- The amount is small so you do not think twice, but the form collects your full card number, expiry date and security code.
- After you pay, it asks for an OTP "to confirm". That code may be approving a much larger payment elsewhere.

## Checks that work on any page

1. **Read the registered domain.** It should belong to the company, not include its name as decoration. Our guide on [how to spot a phishing website](https://elevven11studio.github.io/blog/how-to-spot-a-phishing-website/) explains how.
2. **Check how you got here.** Logging in from a bookmark, the official app or an address you typed is far safer than logging in from a link.
3. **Look for `http` on a login page.** A password page without HTTPS is a red flag. With HTTPS it still might be fake, so this check only rules things out.
4. **Watch for odd flow.** Real services rarely ask you to log in twice, or send you to an unrelated address straight after you sign in.

## If you already typed your details

Move fast and in this order:

1. **Change the password** on the real site, opened from a bookmark or the app. Change it anywhere else you used the same password.
2. **Sign out other sessions.** Most email and social accounts have an option to sign out of all devices.
3. **Check your recovery settings.** For email, check the recovery phone number, recovery email and any forwarding rules or filters. Attackers add these to keep access quietly.
4. **Turn on two step login** if it was off.
5. **Call your bank** if you typed card, account or OTP details. Use the number on the back of your card, not one from the message.
6. **Expect follow up.** Scammers sometimes call pretending to be the bank's fraud team. Hang up and call the bank yourself.

## How WebGuard helps on login pages

[WebGuard](https://elevven11studio.github.io/webguard/) is a free Chrome extension we built for the moment just before you type. It detects login and payment forms and flags two things you cannot easily see yourself: a form that posts to a different domain than the page you are on, and a password sent over plain `http`. It also checks for impersonation of 34 commonly imitated services, typosquatting, lookalike characters and a page title that disagrees with the domain.

It never reads what you type into password fields. Its popup includes a password checker that rates strength, spots common passwords and keyboard runs, and can check breach lists if you turn online checks on. Even then, only the first five characters of a hash of the password are sent, so the service cannot learn the password.

Verdicts are Low Risk, Suspicious, High Risk or Unknown, and only High Risk interrupts you. It is not a password manager or an antivirus, and it cannot promise a page is genuine. Use it alongside the password manager habit above, not instead of it.

## Keep it simple

Log in from bookmarks and apps, notice when autofill goes quiet, and never hand over an OTP to a page you reached from a message. If you slipped, change the password from the real site first and work down the list.

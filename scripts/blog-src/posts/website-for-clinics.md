People rarely browse clinic websites for fun. They arrive with a worry: a child with a fever, a tooth that hurts, a knee that has not been right since a match. They want to know quickly whether you can help, when you are open and how to reach a person.

That shapes everything about a good clinic website. It should be calm, plain and fast to scan. This post covers general website guidance only, not medical, legal or regulatory advice. Check what your professional body allows you to publish.

## The four questions every patient asks

1. **Do you treat what I have?**
2. **Are you open now, or when?**
3. **Roughly what will it cost, and do you take my HMO?**
4. **How do I book or talk to someone?**

If your homepage answers all four without scrolling far, you are ahead of most clinics.

## What belongs on the page

| Section | What to include | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Services | Short, plain names with one line each | Patients search by problem, not by department |
| Opening hours | Each day listed, plus what changes on weekends | The most checked detail on the site |
| Prices | "From" prices for common visits | Reduces calls that only ask about cost |
| Insurance | Whether you accept HMOs and which ones | A deciding factor for many families |
| Booking | A way to request a time | Turns a visit to the site into an appointment |
| Contact | A call button and WhatsApp | Some patients need to speak to someone now |
| Common questions | Referrals, walk-ins, waiting times | Answers the same questions your front desk gets daily |

## Write like the receptionist, not the textbook

"General consultation: fever, infections, aches and the things you are not sure about" is clearer than a list of clinical terms. Patients who are anxious read less, so keep each service to a sentence.

Be specific about hours. "Saturday 9am to 4pm, lab closes at 2pm" saves someone a wasted trip. If you see walk-ins, say so, and say whether booking gets them seen sooner.

## Things to leave off

- **Promises about outcomes.** Avoid phrases like "guaranteed cure" or "100% success". They can mislead patients and may break the rules that govern health advertising.
- **Before and after photos without consent.** Only publish patient images with clear written permission.
- **Testimonials you cannot back up.** If you show patient quotes, use real ones, shared with permission.
- **Detailed medical advice.** A website is not a consultation. Point people to come in or call.
- **Asking for more than you need.** A booking request needs a name, a phone number, the service and a preferred time. Do not ask patients to type their medical history into a website form.

## How booking works on a simple site

Our clinic demos use a short booking form. The patient fills in their name, phone, the service and a preferred date, then taps a button. That opens WhatsApp with the request already written, and your staff confirm the time in the chat.

This works well for most small clinics because there is nothing new for staff to learn. It is not a booking system: it does not check a live calendar, block double bookings or send reminders. Those need a database, and the [pricing page](/pricing/) lists booking systems and authentication as work quoted separately.

The same goes for patient portals, online results, records and payments. A static website is the wrong place for anything private. Keep those in proper clinical software.

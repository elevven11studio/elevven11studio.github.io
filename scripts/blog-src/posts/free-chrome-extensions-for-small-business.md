A browser extension is a small add-on that gives Chrome an extra job. The right one saves you time every day. The wrong one can read everything you do online.

Below are the four free extensions we build at Elevven11 Studio, who each one is for, and a short checklist for installing any extension without regret.

## What all four have in common

They follow the same rules, so knowing one tells you what to expect from the others:

- **Checks run in your browser.** No account, no login and no server to upload to.
- **No analytics or telemetry.** Nobody at the studio can see what you browse or what the tools find.
- **Free, with no paid tier or trial.** Each shows one labelled house advert for the studio's own work, and one click turns it off for good.
- **Permissions explained.** Each extension lists every permission it uses and why, in its settings page and in its own privacy policy.

All four need Chrome 116 or later.

## WebInspect: understand any website

**Who it helps:** anyone who owns a website, is paying someone to build one, or wants to learn from a competitor's.

Open a site and press Ctrl Shift U. WebInspect gives you a report in eight sections: overview, technology, SEO, accessibility, performance, mobile, security configuration, and content and links. Each finding shows the evidence behind it, and you can highlight it on the page or export the report as JSON or CSV.

It is useful for checking that a new website has a page title, a meta description and alt text on its images before you sign off on it. It also reports which parts it could not check, rather than counting them as failures.

Read more on the [WebInspect page](/webinspect/) or in our guide on [how to check what a website is built with](/blog/how-to-check-what-a-website-is-built-with/).

## WebGuard: spot phishing before you type

**Who it helps:** anyone who logs into bank, email or payment accounts, and staff who handle customer details.

WebGuard checks pages as they load for the signs of phishing: lookalike domains, brand impersonation, forms that send your details to a different site, and passwords sent without HTTPS. It gives one of four verdicts: Low Risk, Suspicious, High Risk or Unknown. Only High Risk interrupts you.

It also lets you select text and press Ctrl Shift U to check it for passwords, API keys or card numbers before you paste it somewhere. Online checks are off by default, and password fields are never read.

WebGuard is clear about its limits. It is not an antivirus or a password manager, and it never calls a site "safe". See the [WebGuard page](/webguard/) and our guide on [how to spot a phishing website](/blog/how-to-spot-a-phishing-website/).

## SiteExtract: save a page as a starter project

**Who it helps:** designers, developers and business owners briefing a designer.

SiteExtract saves the page you are viewing as one ZIP of HTML, CSS, images, fonts and design tokens such as colours and spacing. Unzip it and open index.html, and it works offline. A report lists anything that could not be downloaded and why.

It is meant for learning, prototyping and sites you have the right to copy. The text, images and fonts still belong to their owners. More on the [SiteExtract page](/siteextract/) and in [turn a web page into a starter project](/blog/turn-a-web-page-into-a-starter-project/).

## ShopInspect: check a product before you buy

**Who it helps:** anyone buying stock or equipment online, from Jumia and Konga to Amazon, AliExpress and Shopify stores.

Open a product page and press Ctrl Shift Y. ShopInspect checks whether the advertised discount matches the prices shown, summarises what buyers praised and complained about, shows who the seller is, and notes what the listing leaves out, such as a return window or warranty. It gives a verdict from Excellent Buy to Poor Value, or Not Enough Information when the listing says too little.

It cannot tell you whether the price is good compared with other stores, and it says so. See the [ShopInspect page](/shopinspect/) and [how to check a product listing before you buy](/blog/how-to-check-a-product-listing-before-you-buy/).

## How to install any extension safely

These steps apply to any extension, not just ours.

1. **Install from the Chrome Web Store.** Avoid extensions offered as a download on a random website or sent to you in a message.
2. **Check the publisher.** Look at who made it and whether they have a real website and contact details. A copycat can use a similar name and icon.
3. **Read the permissions before you click Add.** Chrome shows what the extension can access. An extension that only works on pages you open should not need to read and change data on every site you visit. If the request seems bigger than the job, skip it.
4. **Read the privacy policy.** It should say what the extension reads, what it stores and whether anything is sent to a server. No policy at all is a warning sign.
5. **Look at reviews and the last update date.** Recent, specific reviews tell you more than a high star count. An extension abandoned for years may not keep up with Chrome.
6. **Keep the list short.** Every extension is something that could go wrong. Remove the ones you no longer use from chrome://extensions.
7. **Watch for changes.** If an extension suddenly shows ads, opens new tabs or asks for new permissions after an update, remove it.

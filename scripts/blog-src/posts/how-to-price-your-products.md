Many small business owners set prices by looking at what the shop next door charges and taking a little off. Sometimes that works. Often it means selling a lot and still having nothing left at the end of the month.

A good price does two jobs. It covers every cost you have, and it is a price customers will pay. Here are three ways to get there, and how to check the result.

## Start with your full cost

Before you choose any method, know what one item really costs you. Most people count only what they paid the supplier. That is the first mistake.

Add up everything it takes to get one item onto your shelf:

- The supplier price.
- Transport from the market or warehouse, divided by the number of items in the load.
- Packaging, nylon, labels or boxes.
- Bank or POS charges if you pay for stock that way.
- Any market levy or loading fee you pay per trip.

Say you buy a pair of slippers for NGN 4,000. Transport works out to NGN 300 per pair and packaging to NGN 200. Your real cost is **NGN 4,500**, not NGN 4,000.

Then there are costs you pay whether you sell or not: rent, salaries, electricity, data. If your rent is NGN 60,000 a month and you sell about 200 items a month, each item has to carry NGN 300 of rent. Leave that out and your "profit" is paying the landlord.

## Method 1: cost-plus pricing

Take your full cost and add a fixed percentage on top. This is called markup.

With the slippers at NGN 4,500 and a 40% markup:

1. 40% of 4,500 is NGN 1,800.
2. Selling price is 4,500 plus 1,800, which is **NGN 6,300**.
3. Take away NGN 300 for rent and you keep NGN 1,500 per pair.

Cost-plus is simple and safe because every sale covers its cost. Its weakness is that it ignores the customer. If similar slippers sell for NGN 5,500 nearby, your NGN 6,300 may sit on the shelf.

Remember that a 40% markup is not a 40% margin. On the slippers, NGN 1,800 is about 28.6% of the selling price. If the difference is new to you, read [profit margin vs markup](/blog/profit-margin-vs-markup/).

## Method 2: competitor pricing

Look at what others charge for the same thing and price close to them. This makes sense for items customers compare easily, like drinks, provisions or phone accessories.

Do the research properly:

- Check three or four sellers, not one.
- Compare the same size, brand and quality.
- Note what is included. Free delivery or a warranty changes the real price.

Then check the number against your full cost. If the market price is below what the item costs you, matching it means losing money on every sale. Either find a cheaper supplier, cut a cost, or stop stocking that item.

## Method 3: value pricing

Some customers pay more for things that save them time, worry or embarrassment. Value pricing sets the price by what the product is worth to the buyer, not by what it cost you.

It suits:

- Custom work, like tailoring, cakes or event decoration.
- Fast service, such as same-day delivery or repairs while the customer waits.
- Items that are hard to find, or that you check and guarantee.

A cake that costs NGN 15,000 to make can sell for far more if it is for a wedding and arrives on time looking right. The customer is paying for the result. Your full cost is still the floor, but the customer sets the ceiling.

## Use the three together

The methods work best combined:

1. Work out your full cost. That is the lowest you can go.
2. Check competitors. That shows what customers expect.
3. Ask what makes yours better or easier to buy. That tells you whether you can charge above the market.

## Test a price before you commit

You do not have to guess. Change the price of one product and watch what happens for a few weeks.

Suppose you sell 100 pairs of slippers a month at NGN 6,300, making NGN 1,800 each before rent. That is NGN 180,000. If you raise the price to NGN 6,800, you make NGN 2,300 a pair. To earn the same NGN 180,000, you only need to sell 79 pairs, because 180,000 divided by 2,300 is about 78.3.

So you can lose up to 21 sales a month and still be no worse off.

Some tips for testing:

- Change one product at a time, so you know what caused the result.
- Keep notes of sales before and after.
- Watch how customers react. A few questions about the price is normal. Everyone walking away is a signal.
